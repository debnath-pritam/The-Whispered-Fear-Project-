From The City/Town Of

Kokrajhar

The Drowning Whisper of Kokrajhar

Kokrajhar, a town where the mist clings to the skin like a lover's cold breath, sat quietly on the banks of the weary river Sankosh. The evenings in this remote corner of Assam came early, and the nights stretched on longer than they should, filled with an eerie silence that felt more like listening to the heartbeat of something buried beneath the earth. The people of Kokrajhar knew better than to stay out after dark—especially near the river. They whispered stories of the **Bura Bongoh**, the "Old Drowners," vengeful spirits who never made it to the other side.

But these were just tales, weren't they?

Not for Mira.

Mira had returned to her ancestral home after years in Delhi, a city whose relentless chaos had sucked the spirit out of her. Kokrajhar was supposed to be a refuge, a place where she could escape the noise, the traffic, and the gnawing emptiness. She didn't believe in ghosts, or spirits, or anything her grandmother used to talk about as she sat on the verandah chewing betel nut and staring at the river's edge. The stories were just a village's way of making sense of tragedy, Mira thought.

But her first night back, something didn't feel right.

The old house groaned as it always had, the bamboo creaking with each gust of wind. Yet there was a stillness, a suffocating kind of quiet that seemed to wrap around her throat. Mira tried to ignore it, pouring herself a cup of tea as she unpacked. It was then that she heard it—soft, like the murmur of someone lost in thought.

A whisper.

“Ei, ei...” The voice was faint, almost drowned out by the wind, but it was there. It felt like it was coming from the walls, from the floorboards beneath her bare feet. She shook her head, dismissing it as a product of her imagination, the leftover tension from her long journey. But as she prepared for bed, the whisper grew louder, and this time, it was clearer.

“Mira…”

Her heart thudded in her chest. She glanced toward the open window, but the night outside was pitch black. Only the vague outline of the Sankosh River glimmered under the crescent moon, far too distant for anyone to be standing by her house.

She shut the window and pulled the curtains tight.

The next morning, Mira wandered into town, asking the locals about the house she had grown up in. Some of them were wary of her questions, others a little too eager to share old superstitions, but all seemed to mention one thing—**the river.** It had always claimed lives. Fishermen who didn't return, women washing clothes who slipped and vanished under its deceptively calm surface. But it was not the living who haunted Kokrajhar.

It was the ones the river refused to keep.

That evening, as the sun set over the hazy landscape, Mira decided to confront her fear. She walked to the edge of the riverbank, its slow current now dark and sluggish. The air was heavy, thick with moisture, and the quiet weighed down on her, almost pressing her into the ground.

And then, she heard it again.

“Mira…”

The voice drifted from the water, more distinct than before. Mira's feet were rooted to the spot, her breath shallow and fast. She scanned the surface of the river, but there was no one. Only the gentle ripple of water against the rocks. But the whisper kept coming.

“Mira… why did you leave?”

Her blood ran cold. She hadn't told anyone in Kokrajhar about her past—about the night her brother had gone missing near the very spot she was standing. He had been only nine, a child. One moment playing by the river, the next… gone. No one ever found his body. Her family had always believed he drowned, pulled under by the river's cruel current. But why was she hearing his voice now?

“No…” she muttered, stepping back. “No, it can't be…”

But the voice grew louder, more insistent, and now it wasn't just one voice. A chorus of them, layered on top of one another, rising from the water, from the trees, from the very air around her. They sounded like children, their voices sad, lost, and angry.

“You left us, Mira… why didn't you save us?”

Her heart pounded so hard she thought it might burst. The river, once still, began to churn, bubbles rising to the surface. Something—**someone**—was coming up from the depths. Mira stumbled back, her legs weak, but her eyes were locked on the water.

A hand.

Small, pale, bloated from years underwater. Another hand. And another. Their bodies began to rise, water pouring from their mouths and nostrils, their eyes hollow, black pits. Children, dozens of them, reaching out, crawling out of the river. They were wet, and cold, and silent now—except for their eyes.

“You left us…”

Mira screamed, but her voice was swallowed by the night, by the river, by the shadows that surrounded her. The children moved closer, their fingers like icy hooks, grasping at her clothes, pulling her toward the water's edge.

“We waited… we waited for you…”

She fell backward, the mud slick beneath her feet, and as she struggled to get up, one of the children's hands grabbed her wrist—its grip unnaturally strong.

Mira's scream pierced the still night as she was dragged, inch by inch, back toward the river. The children did not speak anymore, but their eyes—those hollow, endless eyes—said everything. They were not going to let her go.

As her feet hit the water's edge, she felt the cold creep up her legs like vines, pulling her deeper, swallowing her screams. And then the silence returned to Kokrajhar.

The next morning, the villagers found nothing but footprints in the mud leading to the river. They never saw Mira again.

But on some nights, when the mist is thick, and the Sankosh runs slower than usual, they say you can still hear her voice—mingling with the others.

Whispering. Waiting.
