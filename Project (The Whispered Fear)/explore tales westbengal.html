The Hollow Eyes of Durgapur

Durgapur, nestled in the heart of West Bengal, is not the kind of place where things happen quickly. Life moves with the slow hum of its steel factories and the steady current of the Damodar River. The town, quiet and industrious, has long lived in the shadows of its own history, a place where the soil still holds the memories of times better left undisturbed.

But there is a place in Durgapur that the locals avoid, a place whose name is whispered only in passing—the Rashmoni Bari.

The old mansion, long abandoned, stood at the edge of the forest near the riverbank, its crumbling walls wrapped in the embrace of wild banyan trees, their roots like fingers digging deep into forgotten secrets. Rashmoni Bari had once been the pride of a wealthy zamindar family, but no one lived there now. The doors were sealed shut, the windows blackened from decades of neglect. And no one, no one, dared enter after sunset.

Except for Rina.

Rina wasn't afraid of old stories. She had always been different from the other girls in Durgapur, curious in ways that made her seem strange to the more traditional families in town. She had heard all the tales growing up—about the pishachini, the bloodthirsty spirit that was said to haunt the old mansion, about the zamindar's family that had vanished one night without a trace, and about the strange cries that echoed from the house on certain nights.

But she didn't believe in ghosts. Those were just the leftovers of a village's overactive imagination, she thought. So when a dare was put forward among her friends one evening—to enter Rashmoni Bari and spend a night there—Rina was the first to accept.

“You won't last an hour,” her friends had teased. “You'll run back screaming like the others.”

Rina only smiled. “We'll see.”

That evening, just as the sun dipped behind the smokestacks, casting an orange glow over the town, Rina made her way to Rashmoni Bari. The house loomed over her, its shadows long and menacing against the dying light. As she stood in front of the massive iron gates, she noticed something unsettling—the air around the mansion felt different. Thick. Heavy. Almost as if the atmosphere itself was pressing down on her, trying to keep her away.

But she wasn't one to back down.

The gates creaked open with surprising ease, as if they had been waiting for her. The overgrown path that led to the front door was choked with weeds, but the path itself seemed strangely clear, as though something—or someone—still used it.

She reached the front door and hesitated for only a moment before pushing it open. The door groaned, and inside, the air was stale, thick with the scent of decay and dust. The fading light from the outside barely reached into the house, casting the interior in shadows.

Rina stepped inside.

The house was deathly silent. No sounds of birds, no distant hum of the town, only the faint creak of the wooden floorboards under her feet. She found herself standing in a grand hallway, lined with portraits—ancient, faded paintings of people long dead, their eyes dark and hollow.

Her heartbeat quickened, but she pressed on. Her friends would never let her hear the end of it if she chickened out now.

Rina made her way through the house, exploring the dusty, decaying rooms. The furniture was still there, covered in moldy sheets, as if the family had just up and left one day, leaving everything behind. But it wasn't until she reached the drawing room that she felt it—a presence.

The room was colder, the kind of cold that gnaws at your bones. In the center of the room stood a grand mirror, its once-golden frame tarnished and dull. Rina caught her reflection in the glass—pale and small in the vast, empty room. She stepped closer to the mirror, her breath fogging up the surface.

And then she saw it.

Behind her reflection, just for a second, a figure stood—a woman, her skin gray and cracked like dried earth, her hair matted and hanging down her face, covering her eyes. Rina spun around, but the room was empty.

Her heart raced. “It's just my mind playing tricks,” she whispered to herself.

But then the whisper came.

“Rina…”

The voice was soft, almost delicate, as if it were coming from inside her own head. She backed away from the mirror, her breath catching in her throat. Her body felt heavy, as though something unseen had wrapped itself around her, holding her in place.

“Rina… why did you come?”

The voice was closer now, more insistent. She could feel it on the back of her neck, cold and damp, like breath from something long dead.

Suddenly, the room went completely dark. The last rays of light from the setting sun had disappeared, leaving her in total blackness. Panic surged through her veins. She fumbled for her phone, her hands trembling as she switched on the flashlight.

And then she saw it.

The portraits on the walls—the people in them—were watching her. Their eyes, once lifeless and painted over, were now sharp, glinting in the beam of her flashlight. Each set of eyes followed her, unblinking, filled with something far more sinister than just malice.

The whispers grew louder.

“Rina… Rina…”

She turned to run, but the house seemed to shift around her, the hallways narrowing, the doors closing on their own. Her footsteps echoed unnaturally loud, almost as if the walls were swallowing the sound. Her breath came in ragged gasps as she stumbled back toward the main hallway, toward the front door.

But the door was no longer there.

Instead, in its place stood her—the woman from the mirror. Only now, she was closer, her face visible in the dim light of Rina's phone. The woman's eyes were gone, empty sockets oozing black fluid that dripped down her cracked cheeks. Her mouth twisted into a grotesque smile.

“Stay,” the woman whispered, her voice a ragged hiss. “Stay with us.”

Rina screamed, stumbling backward, but the air around her had thickened, like invisible hands pushing against her. The woman reached out, her fingers long and bony, nails blackened and broken, moving with a slow, deliberate hunger.

Just as the woman's hand touched her, Rina felt the cold burn of it seeping into her skin, freezing her blood. Her body was paralyzed, her mind screaming for escape, but her limbs would not obey. The woman leaned in, her breath hot and foul against Rina's ear.

“Once you enter Rashmoni Bari, you never leave,” the voice rasped, and then, with a final, cold whisper—"You belong to us now."

The next morning, Rina's friends found the gates of Rashmoni Bari wide open. They called for her, their voices trembling with both guilt and fear, but there was no answer. Only the wind, carrying faint whispers from the depths of the house, and a chill that crept up their spines.

Rina was never seen again.

But on certain nights, when the wind is just right and the moon hides behind thick clouds, the people of Durgapur say you can hear her—whispering from inside Rashmoni Bari.

Calling for help.
